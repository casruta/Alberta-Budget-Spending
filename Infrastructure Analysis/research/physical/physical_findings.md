# Physical infrastructure evidence: Alberta, 2015–16 through 2024–25

Scope: physical output evidence from official Alberta consolidated annual reports already present in this repository. Despite filenames containing “Budget”, these PDFs report final actual results. The UCP government began April 30, 2019; 2019–20 is a transition fiscal year, while 2015–19 is the preceding NDP period. Earlier Progressive Conservative government projects can appear in both periods. Completion during a government is not proof that government initiated or caused a project.

`physical_metrics_evidence.csv` and JSON contain 25 directly sourced observations, exact text excerpts, caveats, and **one-indexed physical PDF pages**. Older reports can have different printed footer numbers. Health-specific evidence is separately documented in `health/`. Text files are mechanically extracted local source copies, not newly sourced government reports. Alberta/AHS websites were blocked by network CONNECT 403 on October 9, 2026, so claims not supported by the local documents have been left unverified.

## What actually changed

### Schools: deliveries and modernization, but no defensible province-wide net-seat growth estimate

Annual reports list 19, 20, 15, 16, 14, and 12 completed school projects in 2019–20 through 2024–25, respectively. The arithmetic total is 96 **reported project completions**, not 96 new schools. This series includes replacements, additions, modernization, and even planning/design-only entries: David Thompson School design in 2019–20; Montgomery planning and Lewis Farms design in 2020–21; Chinook Learning Centre planning in 2021–22. A chart must label this as reported project completions and explicitly state this counting limitation. Prefer named examples or student-space indicators over implying the count is physical building additions.

For 2021–22 the report distinguishes **5,000+ new student spaces** from **5,900+ modernized spaces** (PDF p20). For 2024–25 it gives **11,000+ new and modernized spaces combined** from completed projects, plus 60,000+ new and modernized spaces in progress (p20). The combined 2025 measure cannot be compared to 2022 new spaces alone, counted as net capacity, or divided by provincial population to prove school provision kept pace. Modernization can improve existing seats without adding seats, and replacements can retire older seats.

Named 2024–25 completions include Elder Dr. Francis Whiskeyjack School and Father Michael McCaffery Catholic High School in Edmonton, Ohpaho Secondary in Leduc, Horseshoe Crossing High School in Langdon, Prairie Winds Secondary in Coaldale, Harry Balfour in Grande Prairie, and Iron Ridge Secondary in Blackfalds (p23). The report also includes existing-school modernization and Fort Vermilion phase work. It reports 87 projects in progress versus 57 a year earlier; this establishes a larger pipeline, not delivered capacity.

The earlier NDP-era reports give a different cumulative measure: 116 of 234 announced projects finished as of 2016–17, 177 of 259 as of 2017–18, and 207 of 278 approved since 2011 as of 2018–19. The denominator and cohort expand, so these are not directly comparable to annual completion counts. They demonstrate that considerable construction preceded 2019 and many later completions inherited a pipeline. Do not calculate an annual growth rate by subtracting incompatible cohorts without project-level reconciliation.

School building stock reporting shifts from 2,000+ in 2020–21 and 2021–22 to 1,750+ from 2022–23. These rounded, lower-bound counts have no reconciled scope change. They do not establish 250 closures or an actual stock decrease. The modular count changes from 5,900+ to 6,000+, also insufficient for an exact growth rate.

### Roads and bridges: concrete expansions and maintenance; no verified aggregate network growth

Before the UCP period, the annual reports document rehabilitation of 860 km of road in 2015–16 and 1,240 km in 2016–17, completion of Edmonton's Anthony Henday Drive in October 2016, and completion of the 240-km Highway 63 twinning corridor in May 2016 (2016–17 PDF p21). These show baseline infrastructure expansion and maintenance already underway; 240 km is the entire corridor, not construction added in that fiscal year.

During the UCP period:

- **Highway 43X Grande Prairie bypass**: 2019–20 completed-project section describes construction of 12 km of four-lane highway and a Highway 43/43X roundabout (p19). The 2022–23 completed section separately records final-stage paving of the Highway 43 Grande Prairie bypass (p23). These staged records should not be added as two separate complete new corridors.
- **Peace River bridge twinning** appears in the 2020–21 completed section (p23): a second bridge north of the existing bridge. That report's declared completed-project definition is operational, but follow-up independent opening verification remains unavailable.
- **Highway 834 Tofield bypass** appears as completed in 2021–22 (p23), intended to separate heavy industrial loads from local traffic and reduce delay. Purpose is not measured outcome.
- **Highway 15 twinning and North Saskatchewan River bridge**, Edmonton to Fort Saskatchewan, appear in the 2022–23 completed section (p23). This constitutes targeted capacity expansion rather than simply resurfacing.
- **Calgary ring road**: still ongoing with southwest portion about 95% complete in 2020–21 (p19), west component ongoing in 2022–23 (p22), then 2023–24 completed-project section states construction complete for **101 km of free-flow traffic around Calgary** (p23). That 101 km describes the whole ring corridor, not the length newly added in the west segment or 2023–24.
- **Highway 201 Bow River bridge on southeast Stoney Trail** completed in 2024–25 (p23): existing westbound bridge widened from three to four lanes, plus a new eastbound bridge and pedestrian crossing. The supplied source does not specify the new eastbound lane count.
- Highway 20/Memorial Trail roundabout in Sylvan Lake appears completed in 2024–25 (p23). Highway 3 and Highway 11 twinning and Highway 686 paving remain ongoing (p19).

Maintenance evidence: 830 km rehabilitated in 2020–21 (p21); approximately 50 bridge-construction and over 80 rehabilitation projects completed in 2021–22 (p20); about 21 bridge and over 52 rehabilitation projects in 2022–23; 20 bridge and about 60 rehabilitation projects in 2023–24; 30+ bridge and about 65 rehabilitation projects in 2024–25 (p20). Project counts cannot be converted to kilometres, net bridge additions, condition improvement, or consistent construction output. A replaced bridge can improve reliability without increasing total bridge stock.

The reports repeat approximately 64,000 highway lane kilometres, but **2023–24 calls the identical figure “two lanes equivalent length”** while adjacent reports call it highway lanes/lane kilometres. This is an unresolved unit inconsistency. The bridge stock rises from ~4,600 to ~4,800, but rounding and lack of a reconciliation prevent assigning exactly 200 net-new bridges. Do not draw a constant-network or bridge-growth-per-capita conclusion from these data alone.

### Water, flood protection, and municipal infrastructure

Springbank Off-Stream Reservoir is in the **2024–25 completed-project section** (p23), described as a dry flood reservoir roughly 15 km west of Calgary, protecting Calgary and downstream Elbow River communities. It is flood-resilience infrastructure, not drinking-water capacity. The named source verifies construction completion using the annual-report definition; it does not establish a separate operational certification date or actual damage avoided.

The latest annual report records **$236 million across 125 rural projects** supported by a combined set of programs: Strategic Transportation Infrastructure, Water/Wastewater Partnership, and Water for Life (p15). The 125 count is not exclusively water projects and does not say 125 completed projects or report extra litres/day, treatment capacity, connection counts, or water-quality outcomes. Municipal transfers and provincial water capital spending establish financial support, not province-wide physical outputs.

The municipal grant architecture changed: the 2023–24 annual report says MSI ended with $487 million provided and baseline LGFF of $722 million beginning 2024–25 (p16). The latest actual report gives **$724 million LGFF provided in 2024–25**, with 53% to Calgary and Edmonton and the rest to other Alberta municipalities (p15). The $722 million initial/baseline amount and $724 million actual should not be silently conflated. The approximate transfer increase is 48.7% nominal; using the latest same-vintage July 1 population of 4.685 million for 2023 and 4.889 million for 2024 gives roughly $104 versus $148 per resident, a 42.5% nominal increase. This two-year program transition is not an assessment of the entire municipal funding basket, inflation-adjusted value, or resulting infrastructure growth. Fiscal spending divided by July population is an explicit alignment approximation.

Municipal transit grant funding appears throughout the reports, including Edmonton/Calgary LRT, but these provincial financial sources do not supply a consistently verified transit opening/route-km/passenger-capacity series. Do not classify funded Green Line or ongoing LRT construction as completed infrastructure based on the capital plan alone. A complete municipal stock assessment needs municipality/transit annual reports and construction completion records.

### Healthcare

See `health/health_findings.md` and `health_project_evidence.csv` for Grande Prairie hospital, Calgary Cancer Centre, Misericordia emergency department, Gunn recovery facility, Rockyview redevelopment, and Lethbridge operating-room expansions. The major hospital pipeline includes projects planned or under construction before 2019. Separate acute-care beds, continuing-care spaces, addiction treatment beds, design visit capacity, and rooms. No province-wide net operational hospital-bed growth estimate is supported by these PDFs alone.

## Source contradictions and condition gaps

The **2024–25 annual report p15 prose** places “17 health facility projects” in a completion highlight list, while the **p20 structured table** says 2 completed and 17 in progress. **p23 names exactly two completed health projects**. Treat p20 plus named p23 projects as the defensible completion evidence and explicitly disclose the internal inconsistency.

From 2022–23 onwards the completed-project footnote defines completion as **construction ended**, with possible pre-operational costs still funded. In 2020–21 and 2021–22 it says **operational**. Therefore aggregate completion timing is not perfectly consistent; the Calgary Cancer Centre is listed as construction-complete in 2022–23 even though the report explicitly anticipates public opening in 2024.

Capital maintenance expenditure and counts of rehabilitated projects do not establish whether deferred maintenance rose or fell, whether pavement good/fair/poor percentages improved, or whether asset replacement cost grew faster than deficiencies. No consistent 2015–25 facility-condition or pavement-condition outcome series was recovered from these local consolidated reports. Earlier out-of-period performance measures cannot fill this gap.

## Suitable visualizations

1. A sector project timeline with separate bands for planning, under construction, construction complete, and verified operational opening; show unknown opening dates clearly rather than fabricating them.
2. A school-output panel showing 2021–22 new and modernized spaces separately; show 2024–25 combined total in a different labeled panel. Do not present as a homogeneous time series.
3. A road work chart using kilometre rehabilitation observations only when available, with missing years blank rather than zero; project-count data belongs in a separate panel.
4. A municipal grant transition chart using actual MSI/LGFF amounts, population normalization, and an explicit note that other funding and inflation are excluded.
5. A completeness matrix by sector: financial input, project delivery, net stock, population-adjusted capacity, condition. The last three frequently remain unknown. This is an honest report result rather than missing values to be imputed.

## Defensible conclusion

Alberta delivered identifiable road, school, healthcare, and flood-protection projects after 2019, while maintaining and modernizing existing assets. The available evidence does not establish the total net addition to provincial infrastructure, that service capacity kept pace with population growth, or a causal effect of UCP rule. Pipeline inheritance, replacements, inconsistent reporting units, operating readiness, and staffing are material limits. Spending and completion evidence together can show resource allocation and selected deliveries, but they cannot alone answer aggregate capacity adequacy.
