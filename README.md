# Alberta’s budget: more dollars, uneven results

**Prepared by:** Kacper Ruta · **Evidence reviewed:** October 11, 2026 · **Source snapshot:** October 9, 2026

**Power BI:** [Free Windows Desktop report and setup](Power%20BI/README.md) · [Download offline project](Power%20BI/downloads/AlbertaBudget-PowerBI.zip). Includes all 42 chart topics; Desktop rendering is awaiting verification.

## Executive summary

- **Resources:** From 2018–19 to 2024–25, program expense rose **30.9%** and capital spending rose **19.6%** before adjustment for population and prices. After those adjustments, program spending per resident fell **4.3%** and capital spending per resident fell **12.6%**.
- **Results:** A larger share of completed surgeries met wait-time benchmarks, but patients still waiting were excluded. Rural and remote ambulance response times improved; emergency waits at the largest monitored sites lengthened. Reported education results and housing-support outputs missed targets. Poverty rose on the current measure, while life expectancy recovered from its pandemic-era low.
- **Interpretation:** The overall pattern is uneven: some measures improved while others worsened or fell short of targets. Spending, completed projects and social outcomes measure different things. These data cannot isolate a government’s effect or establish whether services met population needs.

**Scope:** Financial actuals cover fiscal 2015–16 to 2024–25; each outcome below has its own reporting cutoff. All amounts are Canadian dollars; B means billions and M means millions. For counts, k means thousands. **CPI-adjusted** means adjusted for broad consumer prices, not the costs specific to each sector. Seven key charts lead the report. ## Spending

Program spending increased from 2018–19 to 2024–25, but fell per resident after adjustment for consumer prices. Population grew **13.9%** and consumer prices **20.1%** from 2018 to 2024. Together, they raised the population–price benchmark by **36.8%**: the spending increase needed to keep estimated purchasing power per resident steady. The calculation accounts for higher prices across a larger population, rather than adding the percentages.

![Program spending in nominal dollars and CPI-adjusted dollars per resident, showing that their trends differ.](Quality%20of%20Life%20Analysis/figures/01_spending_intensity.png)

| Function | 2024–25 actual | Nominal change since 2018–19 | CPI-adjusted change per resident |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Health | $29.560B | +34.8% | −1.4% |
| Basic and advanced education | $17.197B | +15.8% | −15.3% |
| Social services | $8.462B | +44.2% | +5.4% |
| Other programs | $16.118B | +35.8% | −0.7% |
| **Total programs** | **$71.337B** | **+30.9%** | **−4.3%** |

Nominal means dollars before adjustment for prices. The Consumer Price Index (CPI) tracks broad consumer prices, rather than the exact costs of each service. The adjustment uses July population estimates and calendar-year prices as approximations for fiscal-year conditions.

Education combines basic and advanced education **per total resident**, not K–12 spending per pupil. Functional expenses group spending by service area and differ from ministry operating expense. Accounting changes also limit comparisons over time. [Source: Final Results, PDF pp. 13–14](Budget%20PDFs/2024-2025%20Budget.pdf#page=13)

Education had the largest adjusted decline in the table, while social services gained. These spending comparisons do not measure the number or quality of services delivered.

## Infrastructure

Capital spending rose to **$7.243B in 2024–25**, up 15.0% over the prior year. After adjustment for consumer-price inflation, spending per resident remained **12.6% below 2018–19**, but only **1.4% below 2019–20**. The lower starting point in the latter year makes the decline smaller.

![Nominal and CPI-adjusted Capital Plan dollars per resident over ten fiscal observations.](Infrastructure%20Analysis/plots/02_spending_per_resident.png)

Municipal and health capital categories accounted for **85.6% of the latest $943M net increase**. Actual spending was **12.7% below the budget reported alongside it**; project timing can affect this gap. The Misericordia emergency department opened in November 2023, and Calgary’s cancer centre became fully operational in October 2024. These openings confirm that selected projects were delivered, but total facility capacity does not show the net increase in staffed services per resident. [Evidence and sources](Infrastructure%20Analysis/REPORT.md)

## Health

**Emergency and imaging access worsened.** The emergency wait for a first physician assessment rose from 4.5 hours in 2021–22 to 7.0 hours in 2024–25. At the 90th percentile, nine in ten included emergency visits reached a first physician assessment within that time. This covers the 16 largest sites, not a provincial average.

Over the same period, the shares of scans completed within clinical priority targets fell from 68% to 41% for MRI (magnetic resonance imaging) and from 87% to 80% for CT (computed tomography).

![Emergency-department 90th-percentile waits rose from 4.5 to 7.0 hours, while MRI and CT shares within clinical priority targets declined.](Quality%20of%20Life%20Analysis/figures/08_emergency_and_imaging.png)

**Other results were mixed.** From 2023–24 to 2024–25, the shares of completed cases within benchmarks improved for hips (62.4% to 72.8%), knees (53.3% to 62.5%) and cataracts (59.7% to 62.8%). Patients still waiting are excluded. Metro ambulance response time at the 90th percentile rose from 13.8 to 14.2 minutes over the same period; rural and remote times improved.

Alberta’s life expectancy recovered to 81.53 years in 2024, but remained 0.43 years below 2019. The calendar 2023 and 2024 estimates are preliminary.

In 2023–24, 19.7% of eligible first emergency visits for mental health or addiction involved people without publicly funded service contact in the preceding two fiscal years. This was above the 17.9% target. This measures prior contact with services; it does not measure recovery or all unmet need.

These findings show uneven progress: selected surgery results and life expectancy improved, while emergency and imaging access worsened. [Health sources and definitions](Quality%20of%20Life%20Analysis/REPORT.md)

Results can be reported up to ten months after the period they cover. The measure excludes people with missing or invalid health numbers and those with less than two years of Alberta health insurance eligibility. It does not measure recovery or all unmet need.

## Education

Five-year completion tracks whether students meet Alberta’s high-school completion requirements within five years of entering Grade 10. It was **87.1% in school year 2023–24**, above 2019–20’s 86.2% but below its peak and **88.8% target**. Self-identified First Nations, Métis and Inuit students had a completion rate of **69.7%**, below their 71.5% target. The overall rate includes these students.

In 2024–25, Grade 9 acceptable-standard results were **69.7% for language arts and 51.7% for mathematics**, below their respective **72.0% and 55.3% targets**. The acceptable standard means adequate understanding of core knowledge and basic skills. The reported rates combine subject results, giving more weight to subjects with larger enrolments. Changing participation and assessment disruptions affect comparisons; the rates do not measure literacy across Alberta’s population. [Education evidence](Quality%20of%20Life%20Analysis/REPORT.md)

Completion was above its 2019–20 level, but the latest completion and Grade 9 results all missed their targets. Higher completion alone does not establish stronger tested learning.

![Grade 9 language arts and mathematics acceptable-standard results and their 2024–25 targets.](Quality%20of%20Life%20Analysis/figures/06_grade9.png)

Completion includes diplomas and other qualifying pathways, such as post-secondary study, apprenticeship or specified course credits. Results have a one-year reporting lag: the 2024–25 annual report provides completion results for 2023–24. Pandemic changes to examinations and marks affect comparisons between student cohorts.

From 2021–22 to 2024–25, Grade 9 language arts rose from 69.4% to 69.7%, while mathematics fell from 53.1% to 51.7%. The enrolment-based weights and changing participation mean these are not simple pass rates among test writers. Missing pandemic observations represent unavailable results, not zeros.

## Living standards

Average weekly earnings rose **from $1,148 in 2018 to $1,328 in 2024** in nominal dollars: the amounts paid at the time. After adjustment for the Consumer Price Index (CPI), which tracks broad consumer prices, earnings **fell 3.7%**. By this measure, average weekly pay bought less despite the higher dollar amount.

Unemployment was **7.0% in 2024**, compared with 6.5% in 2018. The rate measures unemployment within the labour force, rather than among all residents. The labour force includes people working and those available for work and seeking a job. Average earnings depend on job types and hours worked; they do not measure the typical household’s income after taxes and benefits.

On the current **2023-base Market Basket Measure**, Alberta’s estimated poverty rate rose **from 10.2% in 2023 to 11.0% in 2024**. This measure compares a family’s available income with the cost of a basket of goods and services needed for a modest basic standard of living. Canada also rounded to 11.0%; matching rounded survey estimates does not prove that the underlying poverty rates are the same. The chart keeps the older 2018-base measure separate because its basket definition differs. [Poverty source](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/t1/tbl1/en/tv.action?pid=1110013501)

![Alberta and Canadian poverty rates shown in separate panels for the 2018-base and 2023-base Market Basket Measures.](Quality%20of%20Life%20Analysis/figures/11_poverty.png)

These measures show higher pay in dollars alongside lower purchasing power and a recent increase in estimated poverty. They do not establish how every household fared or what caused the changes.

## Housing and access

Reported housing support fell **from 2,302 in 2023–24 to 798 in 2024–25**, below the 1,500 target. The latest total combines **388 units and 410 net additional rent-supported households**. The measure can include regenerated units: existing homes that underwent substantial refurbishment. It therefore does not count newly built homes alone or measure the net change in housing stock.

![Combined affordable housing units and additional rent-supported households fell to 798 in 2024–25, below a target of 1,500.](Quality%20of%20Life%20Analysis/figures/10_housing_support.png)

In **2024–25**, **63.0%** of clients admitted to continuing-care homes were placed within 30 days of assessment, below the 66% target. This preliminary result covers admitted clients and excludes people still waiting.

Medical eligibility decisions for Assured Income for the Severely Handicapped (AISH) took a median of **5.3 weeks in 2024–25**. This was within the nine-week target but longer than the 4.3 weeks reported in 2023–24. The median is the middle waiting time; the clock starts after complete documentation, so this does not measure the full application wait.

Childcare fees averaged **$15/day in January 2024** for licensed care serving children up to kindergarten age. In the same age group, **142.7k licensed spaces** were reported as of March 31, 2025, including 14.3k net new spaces. Licensed capacity does not show actual attendance or how many spaces had staff available.

Together, these measures describe housing support and selected access conditions; they do not establish whether services met overall demand. [Sources and scope](Quality%20of%20Life%20Analysis/REPORT.md)

## Safety

Police-reported violent crime in Alberta rose from **1,462 to 1,591 incidents per 100,000 people (+8.8%)** between calendar 2019 and 2023. Over the same period, the property-crime rate fell from **5,894 to 4,752 (−19.4%)**, measured in the same units. These rates account for population size, making comparisons more useful than incident counts alone. [Source: PDF p. 28; methods p. 44](Quality%20of%20Life%20Analysis/research/source_verification/downloads/pses-annual-report-2024-2025.pdf#page=28)

The rates exclude unreported victimization and do not directly measure how safe people feel. The rural and urban groups reflect police-service coverage rather than a simple division by where people live. The chart’s two panels use independent scales, so compare their labelled values rather than the height of their lines.

![Separate violent- and property-crime rate panels for Alberta, with urban and rural police-service comparisons.](Quality%20of%20Life%20Analysis/figures/04_safety.png)

The evidence shows a mixed safety picture: fewer reported property crimes relative to population, alongside more reported violent crimes. These results do not establish that a particular budget or government caused either change.

## What remains uncertain

The report lacks a consistent record of **net, staffed capacity**: services available after accounting for openings, closures and staffing. Sector-specific costs, complete waiting lists and outcomes for different population groups are also missing. Housing-cost burden, food insecurity, life satisfaction and healthy life expectancy remain unresolved.

Spending and selected outcomes therefore cannot support an overall quality-of-life score or show whether services meet population needs. These comparisons also cannot separate government policy effects from other influences. The evidence describes spending and results, but cannot establish adequacy or causality.

## Sources and methods

The reviewed financial source is **2024–25 Final Results**, although its local filename says “Budget”. Spending figures come from that report; the adjustments for prices and population are calculated comparisons. The outcome measures above have their own reporting dates, separate from the financial cutoff.

July population and calendar-year prices approximate conditions during the fiscal year. The Consumer Price Index (CPI) measures broad consumer prices; it does not measure the government’s exact costs of providing services. Adjusted spending per resident therefore helps compare spending over time, but does not directly measure service quality.

<details>
<summary>Calculation, primary sources and reconciliation</summary>

```text
Change in CPI-adjusted spending per resident
    = (endpoint spending / starting spending)
      / (endpoint population / starting population)
      / (endpoint CPI / starting CPI) - 1
```

The calculation compares spending growth with population growth and price growth. CPI is compounded from rounded Alberta annual rates, so each year’s price change builds on the previous year’s level. July population and calendar-year CPI do not cover exactly the same period as a fiscal year, making the adjustment an approximation.

Financial comparisons use one report version: **2024–25 Final Results**, including its revisions to earlier years. Original budget/actual pairs keep the figures published together at the time. Accounting-policy changes in 2019–20 and 2021–22 still limit how closely the years can be compared.

- [Final Results PDF, pp. 13–17](Budget%20PDFs/2024-2025%20Budget.pdf#page=13): spending, population, prices and earnings.
- [Official ministry PDF inventory](Quality%20of%20Life%20Analysis/research/source_verification/current_pdf_inventory.csv): source URLs and hashes.
- [Statistics Canada poverty table 11-10-0135-01](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/t1/tbl1/en/tv.action?pid=1110013501), released April 29, 2026: separate Market Basket Measure (MBM) bases.
- [Life table 13-10-0837-01](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/t1/tbl1/en/tv.action?pid=1310083701), released January 13, 2026: period life expectancy and uncertainty.
- [Financial reconciliation](Quality%20of%20Life%20Analysis/review/current_website_functional_all80_comparison.csv): all 80 selected cells matched the current official download. The source’s $6M component-to-total residual in 2022–23 is retained: its components add to $6M more than the reported total.

Detailed reports contain metric definitions, source pages, raw inputs and remaining evidence gaps. Review covers the current report and selected historical calculations; the full historical archive remains only partly reviewed.
